The official page for [Final Stand: Remastered](https://www.roblox.com/games/7922773201/Final-Stand-Remastered) describes it as a remaster of the classic Roblox RPG *Dragon Ball Z Final Stand*, run by the group Final Stand Remastered. That is the solid ground. The trouble is that most of what circulates about this game — code lists, stat multipliers, drop tables, "best race" rankings — is not on the official page at all. This guide separates what the developers actually advertise from what players observe in-game, and from what nobody has documented publicly.

## Why "Remaster" Is the Key Word

*Dragon Ball Z Final Stand* built its reputation over years, and a remaster carries a specific promise: the same core loop, rebuilt. The official page's own framing — "a remaster of" — is the developers telling you which game to compare it against.

Practically, this changes how you should research the game. Broad structural questions — what races exist, what transformations look like, how leveling generally flows — are reasonably answered by looking at the original game's documented content, which the credited wiki sources cover. What you cannot safely carry over are balance numbers. A remaster can change any value, and the official page confirms none of them.

## Separating Advertised Features From Community Claims

Here is the cleanest way to sort information about this game.

**Advertised by the developers:** Namek is the current expansion. The level cap is 305. The transfer cap is 125. It is a remaster of Dragon Ball Z Final Stand. Servers hold up to 50 players. The team is credited by role.

**Third-party observations:** Leveling routes, which race feels strongest at a given stage, how long a grind takes, whether a particular enemy is worth farming. These appear in community guides and videos, and they are useful — but they are player conclusions, not developer documentation. They go stale with every update.

**Not documented anywhere official:** Promo codes, item pricing, skill tree layouts, drop rates, hotkeys, patch notes, or release schedules. If a source presents any of these as fact without showing an in-game screenshot or a developer post, you have no reason to trust the specific numbers.

## How to Verify Anything Yourself

Because the official page is deliberately thin, verification is a skill worth building. Three habits cover most cases:

**Check the official page first.** The description and the last-updated date are the fastest way to confirm whether the game is currently in the Namek expansion and whether the caps you remember are still accurate. A page that was updated recently is a page whose stated caps are likely current.

**Confirm numbers in the game UI.** Level caps and transfer caps are the kind of thing a game surfaces in its own interface. Look for a level display, a cap indicator, or a transfer or prestige screen. If the in-game number matches 305 or 125, the official page is confirmed. If it differs, trust the game — it is running the live build.

**Distrust anything with suspicious precision.** A guide claiming a specific drop rate to two decimal places, or a code that expires in 24 hours, is making a claim the official page does not support. That does not automatically make it wrong, but it makes it unverified, and you should require a screenshot or a developer announcement before acting on it.

The official page tells you the ceiling — level 305 — and the transfer cap of 125, which is the number that governs how much progress carries across a transfer.
